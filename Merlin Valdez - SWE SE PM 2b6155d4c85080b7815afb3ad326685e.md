- How I’ve successfully identified use cases that moved key metrics
    
     The Sankofa program for unhoused students that I managed had seen a 40% rise in student attendance city wide in the year previous to me taking over as program manager. My main question as actually to 1) Figure out what was the key intervention that drove that change 2) Invest heavily in that intervention.   
    
    - Though we had a end of the year report, we did no have a consistent reporting system. So I built our weekly reporting system based on the main reporting items required by the DYCD, and the data previously reported on.
    - I then trained our team on data-driven decision making for mentoring, and introduced as part of their role, filling out quick and weekly intervention reports.
    - We took the student reporting and matched it against the student attendance data on our partner portal, and started identifying that early morning texts and phone calls saying “I will be there today” had a profound impact on student attendance, even when a mentor did not send a text, a text from another STH student had a similar impact on attendance (it was fun to look at those spikes in the data). So a mentor now would have a direct message thread with their mentee, and a group chat with the mentors, in the morning, you’ll see the group chat light up with the question “Who gone be there”.  Our attendance growth those two years was over 60% for STH students (mind you it is 20% city-wide)

- Examples of partnering with Ops or Data to fix reporting issues
    - When I ran the Sankofa Mentorship Program with the DYCD, our mentors were deployed at schools on a daily basis supporting cohorts of 5-7 students, all of which were in unhoused situations. Our responsibility with the DYCD was to leverage the mentorship relationships as a way to fight chronic absenteeism by this particular student population. Though in the past, quarterly reporting had been done by identifying attendance growth of the students every 8 weeks, we did not have any concrete information of the actual impact of mentoring activities that we could justify in our budget.
    - I created a case management form for mentors to fill out weekly, unlocked a budget to pay them to do it, and trained them on data driven decision making, being transparent about the fact that the data tracking system is each team member’s personal contribution to the financial sustainability of the program and our ability to hire them in the future, and as an opportunity to hone in on our most worthwhile interventions and celebrate those with the team.
        - As a result of this, we now had actionable weekly data that we could interpolate with attendance data and find what actions impacted weekly students
        - We discovered that morning texts, were our most important intervention beyond walking to school with the students, and honed in on ensuring that mentors made texting a part of their daily workflow.
        - We increased student attendance to above 60% the following two years, 90% of our students by  April would be no longer labeled as chronically absent.
        - Also as a side effect, we found that mentors could speak better to every single intervention with students, and could better account for their impact, all because of they were now being paid to write it down.

- Real examples of navigating conflicting priorities between field needs and education goals
    - In the K-12 space most school leaders are looking for a partner that will own the change management process with them, they can see how a product solves a problem their teachers have, but they clearly understand the knowledge gaps their teachers have when it comes to implementing it, and expect professional development to be bundled with the solution.
        - This was a huge point of tension at Planning Period, as my cofounder and I had to deliver the full product roadmap (we managed a contracted team of product designers, marketing consultants, and education researchers) , while splitting my time to effectively manage paid partnerships and pursue new ones.
        - The way that we solved for this was by:
            - Finding consensus on ground truths and acted on them
                - 1 . Schools purchase partnerships not products
                    - We decided that 6-15 institutional partnerships were a worthwhile goal for a team of 1 person sales & CS team
                    - Our funnel was built on implementing partnerships that our partners would brag about with their peers. Feeding more partnerships.
                    - We applied a ramp up approach that adapted to schools and institutions current budget liquidity. 90 minute training to full year engagements, and the size of the engagement determined the place of the long term deal in the pipeline.
                - 2. Schools need to see that the partnership furthers their goals, and sustain learning quality
                    - All of our product updates were aimed at schools complying with, or exceeding with their state standards. Schools will ask for impact research, and we did not have it because the market is completely new. So instead of focusing on trying to sell the impact of AI in education, we focused on helping schools use AI to scale what already works in education. We have users the ability to align to their specific state standards, we gave them the ability to process their curriculum and high quality materials. Our user learning and product was designed to 1) make it easy to bring AI into the teacher jobs to be done 2) Make it easy to implement research-backed practices and curriculum at community level.
                - 3 . The window of budgetary opportunity favors Professional Development. We get paid for training and embed the product.
                    - This was the hardest lesson for us as founders, we really took a traditional outbound approach to sales and no one had the time to speak to us. But in events where we connected with principals and school leaders, everyone express a need and a budget flexibility to help teachers learn how to use AI. This is when we actually clicked on our early revenue model. Get paid to train and then get paid again by having the school purchase the product. The training investment was almost always bigger than the product purchase again, but once you establish capacity at an institutional level the product partnership lives on, and Principals will transition the software cost to their in school flex budget as it is minimal. (this was true to the extent that companies like Google, microsoft bundled AI in their district deals)
